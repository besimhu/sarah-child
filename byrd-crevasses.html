RESEARCH STORY · EAST ANTARCTICA · BYRD GLACIER

A flood leaves
a mark in ice.

Water can move beneath a glacier out of sight. At Byrd Glacier, a large basal crevasse may preserve evidence of a past subglacial flood.

A plain-language introduction to research led by Sarah F. Child and collaborators.

A flood beneath a glacier is difficult to observe directly. But sometimes the ice above it records a response.

In 2006, a subglacial flooding event occurred beneath Byrd Glacier in East Antarctica. It was followed by a temporary increase of about 10% in the glacier’s speed. Airborne radar measurements collected in 2011 revealed hundreds of basal crevasses. Sarah Child and co-authors used particle tracking to investigate when the largest fractures formed.

What is a basal crevasse?

A basal crevasse begins at the bottom of a glacier rather than at its surface. A short-lived acceleration can increase tensile stress in the ice and help form a large basal fracture near the grounding line.

The study linked formation of Byrd Glacier’s largest basal crevasse to the 2006 flooding event. Very large basal crevasses can leave distinctive surface depressions directly above them. By tracing those surface features along the glacier flowline, the researchers built a timeline of past flooding events.

A surface depression can offer a visible clue to a fracture and flood hidden beneath the ice.

What the evidence does—and does not—say

The paper’s key points report that these rapid dynamic changes appear to have no effect on Byrd Glacier’s stability. The authors also note that, in their observations, the large basal crevasses did not appear to develop into rifts that calved large tabular icebergs.

That distinction matters: a dramatic short-term response can leave a lasting feature without necessarily destabilizing the whole glacier. The study shows how satellite feature tracking and airborne radar can reveal processes that are otherwise out of sight.
